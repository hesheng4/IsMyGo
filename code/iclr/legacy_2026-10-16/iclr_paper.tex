\section{Abstract}
Long-horizon agents must choose a memory strategy---keep everything, retrieve selectively, or compress under a token budget---but the accuracy/cost/latency trade-off among these choices has rarely been quantified under controlled conditions. We present a controlled study on a stratified 120-question subset of LongMemEval-S (seed 42), evaluating six memory strategies (full-context, sliding window, BM25 retrieval, retrieval+recency, window+summary compression, and a 3-tier hierarchical memory) at four token budgets (512/1024/2048/4096) with a single answerer model (mimo-v2.6-flash), yielding 21 cells of 120 runs each (2,520 question-level records). Full-context attains the highest accuracy (relaxed EM 0.4750) but costs 104,024 memory tokens and 24.1 s end-to-end per question---about 26$\times$ the tokens and nearly 5$\times$ the latency of budgeted strategies. Among budgeted strategies, no single method dominates: retrieval leads at small budgets (0.283 at 512), while the hierarchical policy scales monotonically to 0.417 at 4096 (95\% CI [0.33, 0.51]); compression starts near floor (0.067) and only becomes statistically indistinguishable from retrieval at 4096 (McNemar p=0.099). Error decomposition shows the binding constraint is reading, not retrieval: gold-evidence recall reaches 0.99 for retrieval at 4096, yet accuracy conditional on coverage is only 0.30. We conclude that no strategy is dominant across accuracy, cost, and latency, and provide a budget-conditioned selection guideline.

\section{1 Introduction}
Commercial assistants and long-context LLMs lose roughly 30\% accuracy across sessions on LongMemEval (Wu et al., 2024), and feeding the entire history is increasingly untenable: in our experiments, verbatim full-context consumed 104,024 memory tokens per question on average---26$\times$ the footprint of a 4096-token memory budget---and took 24.1 s end-to-end against roughly 5 s for budgeted strategies (range 2.7--7.4 s across the 20 budgeted cells). This makes the \textit{strategy} choice---what to keep, when to write it, how much to spend---a first-order systems decision.

Existing evaluations typically compare memory methods along a single axis (accuracy on a benchmark), yet deployments care about several at once: accuracy, token cost, latency, and delivery robustness. Meanwhile, evidence accumulates that no single memory representation generalizes across task distributions (Wei et al., 2026), that write-time compression irreversibly discards information needed by future queries (Zhou et al., 2026), and that relevant evidence does not imply sufficient evidence---top-k retrieval can return jointly insufficient sets (Li et al., 2026).

We therefore ask: \textit{under a fixed token budget, how do the major memory strategy families trade accuracy against cost and latency, and where do their failures originate?} We answer with a controlled grid on LongMemEval-S:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Six strategies, one knob.} Full-context (verbatim oracle), sliding window, BM25 retrieval, retrieval+recency, window+summary compression, and a 3-tier hierarchical policy are implemented as switches over the same answerer, judge, and dataset; only the memory policy varies.
\item \textbf{Four budgets $\times$ six policies + full-context = 21 cells, n=120 each.} All 21 cells reach exactly 120 questions after a completed re-run (2,520 records; zero empty predictions), removing a completion-subset bias that had previously depressed full-context to 0.2917 (pre-re-run value on the 72 completed questions).
\item \textbf{Trade-off quantified.} Full-context wins on accuracy (0.4750 relaxed EM) but is Pareto-dominated on cost and latency; among budgeted strategies the accuracy leader changes with budget (retrieval at 512 $\rightarrow$ hierarchical at 4096), so no budgeted strategy dominates either.
\item \textbf{Failure attribution.} Read-side failure, not retrieval failure, dominates: at 4096 the retriever recalls gold evidence in 99.2\% of questions, but the answerer is correct only 29.9\% of the covered cases.
\end{itemize}
Our findings support H1 (a reproducible Pareto trade-off with no all-dimension winner) and refine H1a--H1c: full-context is a \textit{reference upper bound}, not a deployable strategy; retrieval is strongest per token at small budgets but lags badly on multi-session and temporal questions; compression shows the predicted weakness on knowledge-update questions (0.329 vs. 0.592 for retrieval, 19 questions, reported observationally per pre-registration).

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A controlled, fully re-run 21-cell budget grid with released per-question records; (2) an accuracy/cost/latency Pareto analysis showing where each family wins; (3) a read-vs-retrieval error decomposition with actionable implications (reranking improves offline recall but not end accuracy).

\section{2 Related Work}
\textbf{Benchmarks for long-term memory.} LongMemEval (Wu et al., 2024) standardizes five long-term memory abilities over 500 questions with expandable histories and an index-retrieve-read pipeline; MemoryBank (Wu et al., 2024b), MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023) and Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025) propose production memory mechanisms, and Hu et al. (2025) evaluate memory via incremental multi-turn interaction. We adopt LongMemEval-S as substrate but shift the object of study from \textit{methods} to \textit{strategy families under a shared budget}.

\textbf{Retrieval-based memory.} Sparse (BM25; Robertson \& Zaragoza, 2009) and dense (DPR; Karpukhin et al., 2020; RAG; Lewis et al., 2020) retrieval underpin most write-then-retrieve agents. Our retrieval+recency variant formalizes the common "half budget to recent turns" heuristic and shows it dilutes retrieval slots at large budgets (0.342 vs. 0.408 for pure retrieval at 4096).

\textbf{Compression and summarization.} Streaming compression summarizes history at write time. TPBench (Park et al., 2026) shows compression evicts turning-point utterances; Zhou et al. (2026) argue write-time compression assigns credit over a long horizon and propose read-time curation. Our compression family (window+summary, with relevance-ranked and structured-verbatim ablations) reproduces the negative result that newest-first digests are weakest, while read-side relevance ranking helps but does not close the gap.

\textbf{Hierarchical and progressive memory.} APDMem (Fu et al., 2026) drills from topic summaries to raw messages on demand; Jev-Mem (Jiang et al., 2026) routes between a lightweight controller and a large model. Our 3-tier hierarchical policy is the budget-aware instantiation of this family; its accuracy rises monotonically with budget and it rivals the retrieval family at 4096.

\textbf{Why a controlled trade-off study.} Prior work measures accuracy (or latency) alone; Deng \& Sinchaisri (2026) show final-answer metrics hide stale/misattributed failures, and Li et al. (2026) show relevance ≠ sufficiency. We complement these by reporting accuracy, tokens, latency, and a read/retrieval failure split on the \textit{same} runs.

\section{3 Method: Six Strategies Under One Budget Definition}
\textbf{Budget.} For budgeted strategies, the memory presented to the answerer is capped at \(B \in \{512, 1024, 2048, 4096\}\) tokens (cl100k\_base). Full-context has no budget (dagger in all tables) and is treated as a \textit{lossless oracle upper bound}, per its implementation annotation, not as a deployable policy.

\textbf{Policies.}

1. \textbf{Full-context (verbatim):} entire history is passed; token cost grows linearly with history (measured slope ≈ 0.9997 tokens per cl100k token, r$^2$=0.9999, n=5100).

2. \textbf{Sliding window:} keep the most recent \(B\) tokens.

3. \textbf{Retrieval (BM25):} index all turns; return top-scoring evidence up to \(B\) tokens.

4. \textbf{Retrieval+recency:} allocate \textasciitilde{}50\% of \(B\) to BM25 evidence and \textasciitilde{}50\% to most recent turns.

5. \textbf{Compression (window+summary):} keep a recent window plus incremental digests of older turns up to \(B\).

6. \textbf{Hierarchical (3-tier):} session summaries $\rightarrow$ key facts $\rightarrow$ verbatim evidence, allocated 25/50/25 across recency/summary/retrieval by default (allocation ablation in \S{}6.4).

All policies share the answerer, prompt template, and judge; differences therefore identify the memory strategy itself.

\section{6 Analysis and Discussion}
\subsection{6.1 Where do failures originate? Reading, not retrieval}
Table 5 decomposes each cell into gold-evidence recall (retrieval success) and accuracy conditional on coverage (reading success):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Retrieval saturates.} BM25 gold recall rises 0.814 $\rightarrow$ 0.992 from 512 to 4096; dense and hybrid reach 1.000. By 2048, retrieval failure is nearly gone (retr-fail 0.017).
\item \textbf{Reading does not improve.} Accuracy|covered stays ≈0.30 at every budget (0.312 $\rightarrow$ 0.299) while \textit{read-fail} grows to 0.695---having the evidence in context is far from using it. This is the "relevance ≠ sufficiency" failure mode (Li et al., 2026) quantified on our grid.
\item \textbf{Compression inherits both errors.} At 512 recall is only 0.250 and read-fail 0.225; as recall climbs to 0.900 at 4096, read-fail climbs to 0.750---the same reading bottleneck, reached earlier.
\item \textbf{Window policies fail at retrieval by construction} (retr-fail 0.83--0.92).
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Implication:} at $\ge$2048 tokens, engineering effort should move from better retrieval to better reading (selection, ordering, structured evidence), not to larger k.

\subsection{6.2 A reranker probe confirms the reading bottleneck}
Adding a cross-encoder reranker raises offline R@5 from 0.834 $\rightarrow$ 0.954 yet \textit{lowers} online EM\_rel slightly (0.300 $\rightarrow$ 0.292, McNemar p=1.00); dense bge-base gains +0.025 EM\_rel (p=0.549) and its reranked variant adds nothing (+0.000). Offline ranking metrics therefore do not translate to end-task accuracy here---further evidence that the bottleneck is post-retrieval reading. (These are diagnostic runs at 4096, n=120 per variant; they are within-run comparisons and are not comparable to the main-grid cells.)

\subsection{6.3 Is it the memory format? Structured verbatim compressors do not rescue compression}
Free-form digests (0.158 @4096) are matched by EDU extraction (0.158) and beaten by nothing: key-value extraction reaches only 0.142 (-11.8\% gap-closed). All remain far below retrieval (0.300) and hierarchical (0.308) in the same diagnostic runs. The compression deficit is not a summarization-style artifact.

\subsection{6.4 Ablations (diagnostic runs, read within-table)}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Retriever:} dense bge-small beats BM25 at every budget (0.283--0.325 vs. 0.258--0.297); hybrid offers no consistent gain; recency half-budget is worst (0.211--0.281). Sparse-vs-dense differences are small next to the read-side gap.
\item \textbf{Read-side selection:} relevance-ranked digests lift compression from 0.150 $\rightarrow$ 0.183 @4096; an event-anchored hierarchical leg \textit{hurts} (0.325 $\rightarrow$ 0.150), as does the event/time-aware plug-in variant ($\Delta$=-0.175 [-0.258, -0.092]).
\item \textbf{Allocation (hierarchical):} default 25/50/25 is best (0.305 @4096); summary-heavy 15/70/15 gives 0.292, evidence-heavy 40/20/40 gives 0.275---the default allocation is near-optimal on this grid.
\item \textbf{Summary length:} at 4096 longer digests help monotonically (20-word 0.117 $\rightarrow$ 120-word 0.200); at budgets $\le$2048 the 120-word variant dips slightly below the 60-word one (e.g., 0.108 vs. 0.117 at 1024). Even the best remains below retrieval.
\item \textbf{Plug-in baselines:} fetch-then-summarize is the one clear positive ($\Delta$=+0.150 [+0.058, +0.242] vs. its compression parent); relevance-ranked compression select is +0.033 [-0.017, +0.083] (n.s.).
\end{itemize}
\textit{Note: protected ablation tables were produced by their own diagnostic runs and are compared within-table only; absolute values are not directly comparable to the main grid.}

\subsection{6.5 Full-context delivery robustness}
The archived full-context sweep attempted 120 questions but \textbf{completed only 36} (84 empty deliveries, 80 s mean end-to-end on completed items). The current run completed all 120 (0 empty) at 24.14 s after re-aggregation, which is why full-context moved from 0.2917 $\rightarrow$ 0.4750: 0.2917 was measured on the 72 questions completed before the re-run, whereas 0.4750 covers all 120. This is itself a trade-off data point---verbatim full-context is not only 26$\times$ more expensive but was historically the least reliable to deliver.

\subsection{6.6 Selection guideline}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{B $\le$ 1024:} use BM25 retrieval (0.283--0.342); anything else is dominated or near floor.
\item \textbf{B = 2048:} retrieval (0.350) and hierarchical (0.333) are statistically tied; choose by access budget (retrieval 38.5 ms vs. hierarchical 35.0 ms access, both \textasciitilde{}6--7 s e2e).
\item \textbf{B = 4096:} hierarchical edges retrieval (0.417 vs. 0.408, CIs overlap); both are within \textasciitilde{}6 points of full-context at 1/28 the tokens.
\item \textbf{Latency-critical, accuracy-tolerant:} compression/window answer in <2 ms access time but lose 9--35 EM points.
\item \textbf{Never choose} sliding window for cross-session workloads (budget-averaged strict EM: multi-session 0.000, temporal 0.094).
\end{itemize}
\section{7 Limitations and Threats to Validity}
1. \textbf{Sample:} a stratified 120-question subset of LongMemEval-S (seed 42), not the 500-question set; strata are small (knowledge-update n=19), limiting per-type power.

2. \textbf{Single answerer:} all 21 cells use mimo-v2.6-flash; cross-reader generalization of the strategy ordering is not established here.

3. \textbf{Judge noise:} relaxed EM is LLM-judged; bootstrap CIs and McNemar tests quantify run-level uncertainty but not judge-version drift.

4. \textbf{Full-context role:} implemented as a lossless oracle upper bound; its 0.4750 should not be read as a deployable baseline (and historically failed to deliver 84/120 attempts).

5. \textbf{Budget accounting:} budgets count memory tokens presented to the answerer (cl100k\_base); prompt-template overhead (\textasciitilde{}145 tokens intercept) is excluded by construction.

6. \textbf{Latency:} single-run wall-clock includes network variance; we report e2e medians-style aggregates rather than repeated timing.

\section{8 Conclusion}
Under a shared token budget on a stratified 120-question LongMemEval-S subset (2,520 records, 21 cells $\times$ 120), memory strategy families exhibit a reproducible Pareto trade-off with no all-dimension winner: full-context leads accuracy (0.4750) at 104k tokens and 24 s per question; retrieval wins the small-budget regime; hierarchical scales monotonically to lead the budgeted field (0.417 at 4096); compression and sliding window are consistently weak. Error decomposition attributes the remaining headroom to \textit{reading}---gold recall reaches 0.99 while accuracy|covered stays near 0.30---and a reranker probe confirms that offline ranking gains do not propagate to end accuracy. Future work should target read-side evidence use, extend the grid to additional answerers and the full 500-question set, and evaluate memory-delivery reliability as a first-class metric.
